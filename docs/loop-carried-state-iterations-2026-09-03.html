Ten iterations of your loop drawing

Not ten new grammars — the grammar is settled. Ten versions of your drawing, each changing one thing, each judged on the only question you asked: does the dataflow read?

Your drawing, and what is already decided

Five things below are not up for grabs, because you already settled them and every iteration here obeys them:

SettledWhere
L1 — the loop is the region plus one cable that comes back no state node, no begin/end pair, no rolled-out ghost
Seed and back land on ONE port no sub-slots, no ticks, no sub-dots at merge.pose
Go to the end first, then loop back the back cable's default route: right edge, bottom lane, home
A z⁻¹ chip, not a delay block M2; the region already says “everything inside runs per iteration”
No pills for intermediate values a pill is worth it at a source or a sink, and nowhere else

So what is actually left? Exactly four things, and all ten iterations are combinations of them: how the boundary port says “collection outside, element inside” · where the item cable runs · where the back cable runs · what the two marks say. That is the whole remaining design space, which is why these are iterations and not alternatives.

The ten

Every diagram below has the same Blocks, the same ports and the same values. A difference you can see is a difference in the drawing rule, never in the example.

A  As drawn 77.0

Your drawing with the geometry tightened — the control everything else is measured against.

The boundary port on the header line takes others; the cable that leaves it falls into the body carrying one Pose per turn, with i = 1 on it.

merge.pose receives two cables — the solid seed from estimate() and the dotted back cable — landing on one port, which is the settled φ.

The back cable goes to the region's right edge, down the bottom lane, and home: the routing rule you already picked.

What it costs. The item cable crosses the seed cable at merge.pose's row. It is small, it is in your own drawing, and it is the first thing a reader's eye trips on — the hop is drawn here rather than pretended away.

B  Split-face wall port 89.2

One dot, painted as its two faces: filled outside, hollow inside, with the type change written across it.

The dot is filled on the outside half and hollow on the inside half, with Poses written outside the wall and Pose inside it.

Nothing else changes. The type flip is the unpacking, and it costs no glyph, no legend and no extra object — SystemSketch already models a port with an outer and an inner face.

What it costs. A 14px dot is doing load-bearing work. At board zoom the half-fill is legible; on an exported PNG at 50% it is not, and the two type words are then carrying it alone.

C  Row-aligned item 84.4

The boundary port sits at the row it feeds, so the item cable is one short horizontal run and nothing crosses.

others lands on the left wall at exactly merge.other's height. The long vertical drop disappears and with it the crossing in A.

Every cable in the body now runs left to right. The only cable that goes right to left is the back cable, which is the one that means something different.

What it costs. The port's position is now a consequence of the body's layout, so moving merge() moves the wall port — or breaks the alignment. It also gives up the header corner, which is where a reader looks first for what the loop iterates over.

D  Seed named on the back cable 80.0

The z⁻¹ chip states both facts a delay has: one step late, and what arrives first.

seed = estimate() sits under the chip. This is M2 exactly as you picked it, and the reason is that every mature tool states both facts — Simulink's Initial condition, LabVIEW's Feedback Node initializer, SDF's initial token.

The seed cable is still drawn, so the name is a confirmation rather than the only evidence.

What it costs. Redundant on this board, because the seed cable is right there and comes from a block called estimate(). It earns its place only when the seed is far away or off-screen.

E  The zero-iterations cable 71.8

Draws what the analyzer already derives: if the loop never runs, estimate()'s pose is what leaves.

A second solid cable runs from estimate() under the region straight to encode.pose. Together with the last-value cable it is the exit φ you settled: two producers, one port.

The picture now answers a question the others cannot: what comes out of an empty collection?

What it costs. It doubles the number of long cables on the board for a case that is often impossible. Your own default was always drawn; this is what always looks like.

F  Back cable on the floor 72.4

The return is not a free cable — it rides the region's own bottom border.

The bottom lane becomes part of the region's chrome rather than a cable competing with the body. The z⁻¹ chip sits on the border.

The body area is left completely clear: every cable inside the region is a cable of the loop body, not of the loop machinery.

What it costs. A border that carries data is a new idea in this grammar, and it collides with the footer strip. It also stops being a cable you can select, drag or restyle — which the shipped temporal StyleProp assumes you can.

G  Tight stub 71.4

The shortest possible return: out of the block, under it, and straight back in.

The back cable never travels to the region's edge. It leaves merge's output, drops just below the block and comes back into merge.pose.

The recurrence reads as a property of that block rather than of the region, which is what it actually is.

What it costs. It contradicts the routing rule you already chose (“go to the end first, then loop back”), and with two body blocks the stub has to reach across whatever sits between them — at which point it is the lane again, only messier.

H  Weight change, no pill 78.8

The cable outside is a doubled rail; it becomes a single rail at the wall. The pill is deleted.

A collection is two rails, an element is one. The transition happens exactly at the boundary port, so the unpacking is shown at the place it occurs.

There is no pill on the item cable at all, which frees that cable for a text label later — the interference you flagged as a problem for another day.

What it costs. It says many became one but never says one per turn, and it cannot say which one. It also spends the line-weight axis, which is otherwise free for future use.

I  Binding pill 75.4

The pill stops being an index and becomes the Python binding itself.

other = others[i] on the wire says the name, the source and the per-turn indexing in one mark — the same sentence for other in others makes.

It also names the value, which is what your pill rule asks for: a pill is worth it when it names a source or a sink, and the boundary is a source.

What it costs. It is a wide pill on a short cable, and the whole point of a dataflow drawing is that the wire already says where the value came from. Two carried names means two wide pills.

J  Live values 83.0

Every mark carries the value it actually has this turn.

The header reads iteration 3 of 7, the item pill reads other = others[3], and the back cable's chip reads z⁻¹ = pose₂ — the value that will arrive next turn.

This is the shipped machinery: the delayed cable's = value field already prints on the pill. Nothing new is invented, it is only filled in.

It answers “make the dataflow clear” the most literally of the ten: the reader does not infer the flow, they read it.

What it costs. It only exists while something is running or recorded. On a static diagram the same marks have to fall back to i and z⁻¹, so this is a state of the grammar rather than a grammar of its own.

The most useful finding is that the top three are not alternatives. B changes the port, C changes the routing, J changes what the marks say — they are orthogonal and they compose. The drawing I would actually make is B + C + D: a split-face boundary port, sitting at the row it feeds, with the seed named under the z⁻¹ chip — and J switched on only while something is running. A is your drawing; that splice is your drawing with its three loose ends tied.

Stress-testing the three

Five loops chosen for the ways a carry breaks, not for variety. Each is drawn three times with the body held identical, so a difference is the iteration and nothing else.

S1 · two carried values

pose, best = estimate(frame, gain)
for other in others:
    pose = merge(pose, other)
    best = keep(best, pose)

What it is here to break:

  • two back cables have to coexist without becoming the parallel-arrow bundle
  • the second carry is written by a block the first one feeds
  • both lanes want the region's floor
B Split-face wall port holds

The split dot is untouched by a second carry — it says one thing about one collection and stays out of the way.

C Row-aligned item holds

Row alignment is what makes two lanes survive: with the item cable out of the vertical channel, both back cables get a clean floor lane.

J Live values strained

Two live chips plus a live item pill is three moving strings on one board. It reads, but it is the first thing to switch off.

S2 · the carry is written by a different block

for other in others:
    fused = merge(pose, other)
    pose  = smooth(fused)

What it is here to break:

  • the back cable now spans the whole body instead of hugging one block
  • the value that returns is not the value the reading block produced
  • `fused` is a loop-local that must NOT look carried
B Split-face wall port holds

Nothing about the boundary changes when the body grows; the split dot is a statement about the collection, not about the body.

C Row-aligned item holds

The long back cable is now the ONLY right-to-left line on the board, which is exactly what makes it readable at a glance.

J Live values holds

The best showing of the three: `z⁻¹ = pose₂` names the value at the far end of a long cable, where the reader has lost track of it.

S3 · a carry and a collection leaving together

trail = []
for other in others:
    pose = merge(pose, other)
    trail.append(score(pose))

What it is here to break:

  • one value leaves once, the other leaves as a collection
  • both exits cross the same wall within a few pixels of each other
  • `score` reads THIS turn's pose, not last turn's
B Split-face wall port strained

The wall now carries one marked port and two unmarked exits. The asymmetry invites the question the split dot cannot answer: is `trail` one float or many?

C Row-aligned item holds

Nothing changes for C — its whole claim is about the inbound side, and the two exits stay clear of it.

J Live values holds

`z⁻¹ = pose₂` beside a live `score()` output is exactly the distinction the reader needs: one is last turn's, one is this turn's.

S4 · a carry that is not written every turn

for other in others:
    if other.ok:
        pose = merge(pose, other)

What it is here to break:

  • on a skipped turn the carry must arrive unchanged — no cable says that
  • the guard is a Branch region inside the loop region: two scopes, one cable
  • an empty `others` means the seed leaves untouched
B Split-face wall port fails

A port that says `Poses → Pose` says nothing about a turn that produces no write. The split dot is silent exactly where the reader needs a word.

C Row-aligned item fails

Same silence. Routing cannot express a conditional write, and the guard band is drawn here rather than modelled.

J Live values holds

The only one that can speak: the chip reads `z⁻¹ = pose₂ (unchanged)` on a skipped turn, which is the fact and nothing else. This is the case that earns live values their place.

S5 · nested loops, one carry each

for track in tracks:
    for det in track.dets:
        pose = merge(pose, det)
    best = keep(best, pose)

What it is here to break:

  • two regions, two back cables, two bottom lanes
  • the inner iterable is an attribute of the outer element
  • the inner carry survives across outer turns unless it is reseeded
B Split-face wall port strained

The inner region needs its own boundary port, and its collection is `track.dets` — an attribute of the outer element, not an outer input. The split dot has no way to say where its collection came from.

C Row-aligned item holds

Two regions, two row-aligned entries, two floor lanes and not one crossing. Row alignment is what stops nesting turning into a knot.

J Live values strained

Correct but crowded: five live strings on one board, and the inner chip's value changes several times per outer turn, which no static reading can follow.

What the stress suite actually decided

C survives everything. Row alignment is the only change that keeps paying as the loop gets harder: it is what stops two carries from tangling in S1, what makes the long back cable the single right-to-left line in S2, and what stops nesting turning into a knot in S5. It is also the change that costs the least — it moves a dot.

J is not a grammar, it is a mode — and it is the only one that can speak in S4. When a turn is skipped, no port, no route and no line weight can say “unchanged”. A chip reading z⁻¹ = pose₂ (unchanged) can. That is worth building, and it costs nothing because the = value field already ships.

B is right and small, and it stops paying at the second exit. It answers the iterable/element question decisively on the inbound side and says nothing about anything else — which is fine, because that is all it claims.

The one thing none of the ten can do. S4 exposes it: a carry that is written conditionally has no drawing. The back cable is one line and it looks the same whether it carries a new value or last turn's. Live values paper over it; a static diagram cannot. If golden 10 ever grows a continue, this is the hole.

The editable board

Real Blocks and real semantic connections; the back cables are genuine temporal: 'data', 'async', 'delayed' records with their z⁻¹ pills, not drawings of them.

Why the board's body has two Blocks. SystemSketch refuses a cable from a Block to itself, so pose = merge(pose, other) — a one-Block accumulator, the most ordinary loop there is — cannot be drawn as a real cable today. The board uses merge() then smooth() so every carry is a real record. That is a product gap, not a design choice.
