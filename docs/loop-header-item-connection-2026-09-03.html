What is now fixed

Settled, this roundWhy it changes the drawing
The header is an operator The collection does not pass through the region on its way to a Block. It lands on the header, the same way a Branch's controlling value does, and the header emits the element. The two regions now read the same way.
A real port, not a derived one “100% I want a port myself so I can wire and show the type of iteration” — so the item outlet is an ordinary port on an ordinary cable. It stays hackable; nothing here is computed-only.
Centred title An operator's name belongs over the middle of it. Left-aligned reads as a label on a container; centred reads as the thing itself.
Zero-iterations arm: always drawn, transparent It and the last-value cable are the exit φ, and for valid dataflow only one is live — so the same fade the Branch uses for an inactive arm applies here.

The constraint that decides this. Solid means data, dashed means the async rail, dotted means one turn late — that is the shipped temporal StyleProp, and the back cable on every board below is dotted. So the item cable cannot also be dotted without two lines meaning two different things on one picture. That single fact is most of the scoring.

The ten

Same region, same Blocks, same seed, same back cable, same faded zero arm. The only difference in each picture is the run from the header's item port.

A  Dotted drop 66.6

Your drawing: the item falls from the header on a dotted line carrying `i =`.

The dotted line matches what you already drew, and it does read as “this is not an ordinary cable”.

The i = pill sits where the eye lands first, at the top of the drop.

What it costs. Dotted is already spoken for. It means one turn late — the back cable on the same board is dotted. Here it means one per turn, which is a different fact, and the reader has two dotted lines meaning two things.

B  Solid drop 86.4

The same run, drawn solid. The item is this turn's value, so it is ordinary data.

The item genuinely is ordinary data inside the region: it is available now, in this turn, like anything else in the body.

It frees dotted to mean exactly one thing on the whole board, which is what makes the back cable legible.

What it costs. Nothing distinguishes the item cable from the seed cable, so the reader learns the difference from the port it comes from rather than from the cable. Which may be correct — but it is a decision, not an accident.

C  Source pill at the port 81.8

A source pill `other · Pose` right at the header, then an ordinary cable.

This is your own pill rule applied: a pill is worth it at a source or a sink, and the header's item port is a source.

It names the binding once, at the place it is created, and the cable stays an ordinary cable all the way down.

What it costs. It is a second object on a board that is already dense at the top-left, and it re-opens the “text on a cable interferes” question one row lower down.

D  Cadence ticks 83.4

Solid, with a short cross-tick every few pixels: one tick, one turn.

A tick is a rate mark, and rate is exactly what is different about this cable. It cannot be confused with the delay's dots because dots are absence of line and ticks are extra line.

It survives a short run — one or two ticks still read — which was your own gate on the async rail.

What it costs. It is a new line decoration in a grammar that already has solid, dashed (async) and dotted (delayed). A fourth is a real cost, and at low zoom ticks and dashes converge.

E  Half-weight line 82.2

The item cable is drawn at half the weight of a data cable. Nothing else changes.

Weight is free — nothing else in the grammar uses it — and it reads instantly at any zoom without a legend.

It says “one value” against the thick cable's “a whole collection” arriving at the header just above it, which is the comparison the reader is already making.

What it costs. Line weight is the one axis a whiteboard user will change by hand for emphasis, so it is the least stable channel to put meaning in.

F  Ghost fan 61.4

The cable is solid, with two faded copies fanning behind it into the same port.

It draws the actual fact — there are N of these and they arrive one at a time — instead of encoding it in a line style.

It reuses the transparency you just chose for the zero-iterations arm, so faded already means “real but not live right now” on this board.

What it costs. Three lines where there is one value is exactly the parallel-arrow bundle you rejected at the start of this whole thread. It is here because it is the honest opposite of a line style, not because I would ship it.

G  Named cap stub 64.0

A stub off the header ending in a labelled cap, and a matching cap at the consumer. No long cable.

The top-left of the region stops being a junction. Two small caps replace a run that has to cross the whole body.

It scales: five consumers of the item cost five caps, not five cables.

What it costs. It breaks the one thing you said you wanted — wire and show it. A named net is not a cable you can drag, and the eye has to match two words instead of following a line.

H  Port above the consumer 79.8

The item port slides along the header to sit directly above the port it feeds.

The drop becomes short and almost vertical, so the connection reads as “this port feeds that port” with no travel in between.

It leaves the header's top-left clear for the Iterable inlet, which is the thing a reader should meet first.

What it costs. The port's x is now a consequence of the body's layout: move merge() and either the port follows or the alignment is lost. It also implies an ordering on the header that does not exist.

I  Type on the port, name on the cable 81.2

The port says `Pose`; the cable says `other`. Your question, answered.

You asked whether the port name should be drawn differently, and whether we are just showing types. This splits them: the port is a typed socket, the cable is the binding that flows through it.

It matches how every other port on the board already reads — pose Pose is name-then-type — with the two halves separated by the wall instead of by a space.

What it costs. Text on a cable is the interference you already flagged as a problem for another day, and this makes that day today.

J  Operator row 86.2

`Iterable ▸ Iter` side by side on one header line: the header read as an operator with an in and an out.

This is your own sentence made literal — I consider the for-loop header as kind of an operator. An operator has an input and an output, and here they sit on one line with the arrow between them.

It also puts the two ports where a Branch puts its control input, so the two regions read the same way.

What it costs. The header line now carries the title, the turn chip and two ports. With a second iterable (zip) or a tuple unpack it needs a second row, and the header starts becoming a layout language.

B and J are the same drawing seen twice. J is B with the two ports written on one header line — the operator made literal. Take J's header and B's cable and you have one answer, not two. That is my recommendation: the header row reads Iterable ▸ Iter, and the run below it is an ordinary solid cable with the i = pill on it. Dotted stays reserved for z⁻¹, which is what makes the back cable mean something.

If solid turns out to be too quiet — and it might, because the item cable and the seed cable then look identical — D (cadence ticks) is the cheapest upgrade: a tick is extra line where a dot is missing line, so it can never be confused with the delay, and it survives a short run, which was your own gate on the async rail. E (half-weight) is the alternative and reads at any zoom, but line weight is the one channel a whiteboard user will grab by hand.

A, your drawing, scores last of the serious options at 66.6 — not because the geometry is wrong, it is the geometry all ten share, but because the line style collides with z⁻¹. Change nothing but the dots and it becomes B.

Two consumers. All three fan out from one junction on the item cable and none of them tangles — which is the argument for keeping the item on a real cable rather than a named cap. D's ticks do get busy on the second branch; B stays quietest.

zip. Two Iterable inlets and two item ports sit on the header without a new idea, and the header stays one line high. This is the case that makes J's explicit Iterable ▸ Iter row look expensive — with two of each, the arrow stops helping and the ports carry it alone.
